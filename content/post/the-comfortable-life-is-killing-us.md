I recently read Erik Rittenberry's essay [The Comfortable Life Is Killing You](https://poeticoutlaws.substack.com/p/the-comfortable-life-is-killing-you), and I saw myself in it like, a lot. He argues that we live in societies that are safer and better fed than ever, yet many of us feel empty. That's because we have removed the one thing that used to give life weight: real struggle. One line stuck with me:

> Purpose emerges from striving against resistance, not from settling into ease.

It made me think of my long-time favorite quote, the one I keep on my [About](/about/) page: T.S. Eliot's _Only those who will risk going too far can find out how far one can go._ 

Take the [motorcycle club](https://tomccitalia.org) I founded ten years ago. Running it isn't always fun. I have people to coordinate, problems to solve, and occasional seasons when everything seems to work against you. More than once, I've been tempted to step back and let it go. But when things work out (and most of the time they do), the satisfaction is great, precisely because the obstacles were overcome.

The same goes for [hiking](/tags/hiking). A long, difficult multi-day hike always has a stretch where my legs burn, the destination seems farther away, and turning back feels perfectly reasonable, yet I endure. 

[Books](/books-i-have-read/)? Same thing. Not all deserve to be read, and sometimes it is perfectly fine to drop one. But limiting oneself to the easy, entertaining ones is renouncing the challenge, and the chance to improve that comes with it.

As I get older, I enjoy comfort more than I used to. I don't think anything is wrong with that, but I shouldn't surrender to laziness. If the pull towards ease grows stronger with age, then I need to push back a little harder.

Rittenberry is careful to say that suffering isn't sacred in itself, and I agree. But I've come to see the things that demand something of me as the things that keep me alive.
